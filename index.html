INTRODUCTION

“But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you, and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth.” Acts 1:8

In the words of the famous slogan of the Lausanne Movement, God has tasked His whole church to take the whole gospel to the whole world [1].

A critical aspect of His mission for the church is to see the gospel brought to least reached peoples and communities where “Christ is not known” (Romans 15:20) - we call this cross-cultural overseas missions. A long-standing and prevalent way of understanding how this is done is via ‘career missionaries’. These have devoted themselves full-time to evangelize, to make disciples, to plant churches, and to help local indigenous churches to thrive. The importance and viability of such a mode are unquestioned.

However, the work of missions done by bi-vocational missions workers is also important albeit less recognized. These do not do ‘church-related’ ministry full-time, but they go overseas with a missional call and a dual vocation.

They work in a secular profession, trade or area of study as a company employee, business owner, non-governmental organization worker, student etc. They recognize their secular work or study as part of their witness for Christ, not merely as a means to an end.

At the same time, they are intent on investing in work that contributes to the growing of a community of Jesus followers. This includes activities such as verbal evangelism, caring and serving the poor and needy, disciple-making, ensuring protection and justice for marginalized and helpless, church-planting, bible translation work and Christian leadership training.

The bi-vocational missions worker is distinct from the following related categories:

A “tentmaker” is a type of bi-vocational missions worker in paid-employment. They thus have a measure of financial self-sustainability. Not all bi-vocational missions workers are tentmakers.

“Missional Professionals” or “Marketplace missions workers” refer to individuals who view their workplace not merely as a place where they work but also as a mission field. These terms are not restricted to cross-cultural overseas work.

The bi-vocational mode of doing missions has been around for as long as the Church has been in existence. The first bi-vocational missionary was the apostle Paul, who worked as a tentmaker even as he made time to witness in the synagogue (Acts 18:3-4). And since Paul exhorted Christians to walk in a manner worthy of the gospel (Ephesians 4:1, Philippians 1:27, Colossians 1:10) and to imitate his way of Christ-centered living (1 Corinthians 11:1), we may infer that Paul saw the way he plied his tentmaking trade as part of his gospel witness. It may also be worth noting that his fellow tentmakers Aquila and Priscilla were also deeply involved in local church ministry (e.g. hosting a church in their home; Romans 16:3-5).

Closer to home, the national missions studies conducted by the Singapore Centre for Global Missions (in 2009, 2014, 2019 and in 2024) have shown that the Singapore church has been sending out “missional professionals” (sometimes known as “tentmakers”) into the nations from at least the 1990s.

Nonetheless, we have observed that many people including local church missions leaders are relatively unfamiliar with bi-vocational cross-cultural overseas missions. Still less do local churches have clear policies to guide the way they identify, raise, train, and support such missions workers.

This bi-vocational primer is offered to develop the Singapore Church’s appreciation of bi-vocational missions. Besides providing a definition of bi-vocational missions, we offer four archetypes for doing missions in this way with illustrations from real Singaporean missions workers.

We hope that this will inform and encourage this generation of the Church in Singapore, and that we will see more people getting released into the nations to make the name of Christ known.

Many Streams, One Direction.

Why Bi-Vocational Missions?

Bi-vocational missions should not be viewed as a compromise compared to full-time career missions, but as a valid means for Christians to participate in God’s global mission. It is also not a substitute but a complementary mode for missions alongside full-time career missions.

There are good reasons why bi-vocational missions should be considered:

It can Release many Christians who may have good and valid reasons for not pursuing full-time career missionary work, but who may get sent via the bi-vocational route.

Bi-vocational missions may offer Access to nations where entry for career missionaries and public Christian ministry is restricted. These nations are often called Creative Access Nations (CANs). The doors may be open for those who enter on a study, work, humanitarian, or business visa.

Beyond access, there may be Sustained Presence as such visas can grant long-term stay in these nations. Sustainability can also come in the form of income to support the missions worker and his or her family in the field.

The work that a bi-vocational worker does may serve as a Bridge to meaningfully engage with locals. This is especially needful where there is a lack of visible local churches for the indigenous people.

The work itself is a form of Gospel Witness to the locals. Christians who do their work and go about their life in a counter-cultural, winsome Jesus-centered way can influence their non-believing neighbor to ask why they live like that (2 Peter 3:15).

Finally, bi-vocational missions can offer a means of Support for Local Believers. For instance, the missions worker may start a for-profit enterprise that provides employment and livelihood support for local believers. This can be especially needful when locals face the real prospect of job loss and other forms of persecution if they decide to follow Jesus.

What are the theological foundations for bi-vocational missions?

The Priesthood of all believers. We believe from scripture that all Christians are called to minister Christ and to be his witnesses in the world. This work is not confined to clergy or to a specially ordained or professionalized group. It extends to those whose main sphere of activity is in the marketplace. 1 Peter 2:9-10, Acts 1:8.

The kingdom of God covers every sphere of life. Christ is Lord of all creation, and therefore claims lordship over every sphere of life. Likewise, believers of Jesus are called to present their bodies (every part of them) as a living sacrifice, which is their worship unto God. There is no biblical basis for presuming a sacred-secular divide. Understanding this encourages us to dignify our marketplace work - we may view all that we say and do at work as done unto Christ and for His glory. Romans 12:1, Ephesians 6:5-8, Colossians 3:17-24.

Integral Mission. The Good News of Jesus Christ and of His kingdom is expressed both in verbal proclamation (e.g. evangelism and disciple-making) and in demonstration (e.g. loving our neighbor, compassionate action). We do not say that evangelism is primary and that social action is merely a means to an end. We also do not say that Christians are to ‘build for the kingdom of God’ through social action, and have no concern for evangelism. They are both essential components of Christian engagement with the world, just as Christians are called to fulfill the Great Commission and to obey the Great Commandment. Luke 4:16-21, 1 Peter 2:9-12. [2]

We want people to know Jesus and to follow Him. We affirm that the greatest need of humanity is to be reconciled with God through Jesus Christ. Such reconciliation must lead to discipleship within a community of other followers of Jesus. This should be the desired outcome of all missions endeavors, including bi-vocational missions. Matthew 28:18-20.

THE PRIMER & ARCHETYPES

The Primer was developed through the efforts of a working group convened by Antioch21. We sought input from a wide range of sources:

  • A consultation for the primer, comprising over 20 leaders (representing church, parachurch and marketplace) was held in May 2026. A larger gathering of over 80 leaders met in July 2026 to review the primer.

We arrived at four archetypes of bi-vocational missions workers. They should be considered as distinct nominal categories:

  • They are not on a progressive scale. One should not aim to proceed, for example, from archetype 1 to 2 and so on.
  • No one archetype is superior or inferior to the others - they are simply different.
  • The archetypes describe our observation of how various missions workers have found their way to a cross-cultural overseas missions field. They cater to professionals and entrepreneurs, whether for profit or for non-profit enterprises, students, and their spouses.
  • The common factor is that they pursue a dual vocation.
  • They may not be able to cover every possible form of bi-vocational missions (e.g. we will not be saying much about students or home-makers, though they certainly can serve in such a capacity).

Accordingly, we did not include ‘career missionaries’ who engage exclusively in the vocation of ‘church-related’ ministry.

GLOSSARY OF KEY TERMS

Bi-Vocational Missions Worker: One who goes overseas with a missional call and a dual vocation. They work in a secular profession, trade or area of study as a company employee, business owner, non-government organisation worker, student or otherwise. They may also be a home-maker. They recognise their secular work or study as part of their witness for Christ, not merely as a means to an end. At the same time, they are intent on investing in work that contributes to the seeding or growing of a community of Jesus followers.

Tentmaker: A type of bi-vocational missions worker in paid employment. They thus have a measure of financial self-sustainability. Not all bi-vocational missions workers are tentmakers.

Missional Professionals / Marketplace Missions Workers: Individuals who view their workplace not merely as a place where they work but also as a mission field. These terms are not restricted to cross-cultural overseas work.

Cross-Cultural Overseas Missions: The work of bringing the gospel to other nations, especially to least reached peoples and communities where "Christ is not known" (Romans 15:20).

Career Missionaries: Those who have devoted themselves full-time to evangelise, to make disciples, to plant churches, and to help local indigenous churches to thrive.

Creative Access Nations (CANs): Nations where entry for career missionaries and public Christian ministry is restricted. The doors may be open for those who enter on a study, work, humanitarian, or business visa.

Unreached / Least Reached Peoples: Peoples and communities where "Christ is not known" (Romans 15:20) — the primary focus of cross-cultural overseas missions. The Joshua Project [3] for example defines Unreached People Groups as an ethnolinguistic group with 2% or less evangelical Christian and 5% or less Christian adherents (of any form). Least Reached People groups are the same as unreached people groups, but implies that they lie on a spectrum rather than being merely ‘reached’ or ‘unreached’.

Incarnational Missions: An approach to missions work that involves being physically, relationally and culturally immersed in the community that is being reached, as opposed to ‘ministering from a distance’. This reflects how Christ took on flesh and dwelled among humanity as God Incarnate.

ARCHETYPE 1:

THE VOCATIONAL
WITNESS

A person placed overseas, who discovers an opportunity and a growing desire to be an intentional witness for Christ.

1

This archetype describes individuals who did not begin with a clear missionary call or a formed missional identity. Instead, they found themselves overseas through educational pathways, short-term assignments, internships, professional placements, or business expansions - circumstances shaped by God’s providence rather than deliberate missions-minded planning.

Over time, what began as an opportunity abroad deepens. They begin to see their workplace and their relational networks as a context for gospel presence, witness and disciple-making. Their vocation becomes the point of access; their presence becomes the witness.

KEY CHARACTERISTICS

  • No initial missionary call or sending structure.
  • Overseas primarily for work, study, or corporate assignment.
  • Missional awareness and identity grows progressively.
  • Intentionality deepens over time through experience.
  • Witness is primarily relational, within existing networks.
  • Unlikely to have gone through formal sending systems at the outset.
  • May require mentoring to move from passive presence to intentional witness.

How an Unexpected Job Offer Became a Missional Journey.

Case Study 1

About LW & his wife

LW and his wife were well-settled in Singapore with stable careers, a loving home, and an active church life. Relocating overseas was, in their words, “the last thing on his and my wife’s minds”. What unfolded over the years that followed would unexpectedly reshape everything.

Background and Journey

LW had not set out looking for a new chapter. He had a stable job, a loving home, and was actively involved in his local church. Yet, through a series of persistent invitations, a visit to City S, and a surprising turn of events with a revised job offer, they “sensed God’s clear leading”.

"After prayer and seeking confirmation as a family, we stepped out in faith and moved to City S. Throughout the whole process, we continued to witness God’s provision; from securing school placements for our children to finding suitable accommodation.”

Transition and Early Ministry

The family settled into an international fellowship. LW hosted a small group of expatriates and taught foundational "Faith 101" classes for new believers. It was a meaningful start, though largely within the expatriate world they had moved into.

After three years, he was assigned to Kuala Lumpur for over a year before returning to City S. This time, something felt different.

"My wife and I felt led to serve among the local S community."

They were introduced to a Canadian couple hosting a weekly Bible study for young S believers - about 25 to 30 people gathering each Monday evening. The moment they walked in, both sensed it.

"We immediately sensed that this was where God was calling us."

Alongside the Canadian and an American couple, they served the group. What struck them deeply was the hunger they encountered with members travelling long distances, showing up faithfully regardless of weather, pressing in for more of God's Word. Together with their co-labourers, LW and his wife had the privilege of walking alongside these young believers, witnessing genuine spiritual growth and even baptisms in the simplest of settings, including in a bathtub.

Challenges and how it caused them to pivot

After two years, LW was transferred to another country. When he and his wife returned to City S fifteen months later, they found that both the Canadian and American couples had gone home. The group had stopped meeting entirely.

It was a sobering moment. But rather than mourn what had been lost, they reached out. About ten former members were willing to restart the Bible study. This time, however, LW and his wife approached things differently. Recognising the uncertainty of their stay, they were far more intentional. They did not simply lead the group, but they went about equipping members to lead themselves.

"We focused on discipleship, equipping them to lead Bible studies and take ownership of the ministry."

They organised evangelistic gatherings during Easter and Christmas, encouraging members to invite friends and they saw the attendance grow. The diligence the group members showed in preparing and leading the studies themselves was, by LW's own account, remarkable and provoking.

Coming Home and Transitions

After another two years, LW and his wife returned to Singapore permanently. God graciously provided another Singaporean couple to lead the group for a season.

Today, the group continues to meet independently, welcoming new members and thriving without its founders present.

“We often do not know how long we will remain in a foreign country. Circumstances can change quickly, and relocation may come with little notice. Therefore, it is vital to disciple local believers intentionally so that the ministry can continue even after we leave.”

"We stay connected with them till today, encouraging them and praying with them."

Reflection and Encouragement

LW reflects on the journey with deep gratitude and on the two lessons he carries with him.

"The first: engage early in local ministry wherever you are. The second: always begin with the end in mind."

The instinct to settle into the comfort of an expatriate community is understandable. But LW's story shows what becomes possible when a believer looks beyond that circle and asks where God is already at work. And the second lesson is one every bi-vocational witness in an uncertain posting needs to hear.

“We often do not know how long we will remain in a foreign country. Circumstances can change quickly, and relocation may come with little notice. Therefore, it is vital to disciple local believers intentionally so that the ministry can continue even after we leave.”

The group in City S did not need LW to keep going. They just needed to be equipped. That, he would say, is the work.

Case Study 2

About JL

JL, 67, is married with two grown children who are now working and married. A retired engineer, academic, and long-time church deacon, JL spent his career building expertise in Operational Research, never imagining it would one day become a vehicle for gospel presence in City J.

Background and Journey

JL began his working life as an engineer in a multinational company. A scholarship enabled him to pursue doctoral studies in Operational Research, after which he served in an MNC before spending the final fifteen years of his career as a university lecturer. Throughout those decades, he remained a faithful church member - serving as a deacon, facilitating a small group, volunteering in overseas church projects, and going on mission trips.

One year before his retirement, JL began asking a quiet but pressing question: "How can I retire meaningfully?" This sparked an exploration of what he would do with the time and skills he had accumulated and how he could use them for God's work.

An invitation came to visit City J, to explore whether his expertise in operational research could be put to use in a farm social enterprise. It was an unexpected door. JL walked through it thoughtfully and in the spirit of discernment.

Transition and Challenges

There were real concerns to weigh. JL had a health condition that required him to be cautious about extended time away from good medical care. His doctor advised him to limit overseas trips to no more than two weeks at a time, at most three to four times a year. He went - but within those boundaries.

Adjusting to life in City J was not as straightforward as he had anticipated. JL had to navigate language barriers, different workplace norms, and a significant shift from the structured corporate and academic environments he had known for decades to a more fluid, developing context. The pace was slower. The rhythms were unfamiliar.

There were moments of quiet uncertainty. At times, JL questioned his own relevance - unsure of how much he was truly contributing beyond the tasks at hand.

Turning Point

What changed things was not a programme or a project milestone. It was the people and actually getting in touch with their lives.

During his time with the social enterprise, JL was occasionally asked to share during devotions. Slowly, he got to know the people around him - their home situations, their challenges, their struggles. He began to see the community more clearly: the daily realities of life there, the complexity of the environment, and just how difficult it is to minister and serve effectively in such a context.

That understanding did not discourage him. It sharpened his prayers. He began praying specifically for the team he worked alongside and for the needy and marginalised community around them. Back in Singapore, members of his cell group prayed for him whenever he travelled. One member made a point of checking in on his wife to make sure she was doing well.

The support was quiet but steady - a small picture of the church holding one of its own.

Reflection and Encouragement

Reflecting on the journey, JL shared: "It is exciting to see how God can use me to contribute to His Kingdom - bringing and showing His love for the people. I thought I was just filling my time meaningfully after retirement. I didn't realise God had already prepared a place for me."

“It is exciting to see how God can use me to contribute to His Kingdom - bringing and showing His love for the people. I thought I was just filling my time meaningfully after retirement. I didn’t realise God had already prepared a place for me.”

JL did not set out to be a missionary. He set out to be used by God. It was in that posture of availing himself as an offering that God quietly positioned him as a witness - present, relational, and faithful in the place already prepared.

JL's story reflects the providential nature of vocational witness, not only in active careers, but even into retirement. What began as a post-career engagement became an open door for gospel presence, relational witness, and quiet influence over time.

How one software engineer discovered that the marketplace is not separate from missions, but one of the places God calls His people to live missionally overseas.

Case Study 3

“In the same way, let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father who is in heaven.” - Matt 5: 16

About YT

YT is a 32-year-old software engineer and consultant. Over time, YT grew to understand that every Christian is called to live missionally through every aspect of life. The question was no longer whether to participate in missions, but whether God was calling YT to do so locally or overseas. Recognising the many privileges of growing up in Singapore - materially, spiritually, and otherwise - serving overseas felt like a natural next step. After much prayer, YT relocated to Tokyo with three objectives: to be a Christian in the marketplace, to actively serve in a local church, and to explore running a business that could uplift others and bear witness to Christ.

Background and Journey

YT had opportunities to participate in short-term mission trips and even attended a Christian conference in Tokyo, but missions always seemed like something meant for other people. At the same time, there was a strong sense of calling towards the marketplace, and for many years these two callings felt mutually exclusive.

"I always saw it as either missions or the marketplace, not missions at the marketplace."

Over time, that understanding gradually shifted. While attempting to build a side business founded on Christian values, YT realised that, in many ways, this was an exploration of doing business as missions, even without knowing the term at the time.

Around the same period, YT found himself at a crossroads professionally. Through conversations with friends and church leaders, together with much prayer and discernment, YT decided to take a step of faith and participate in God's mission overseas.

Transition and Challenges

Having lived in Japan for only about a year and a half, YT describes this season as still being an early one.

While prepared for possible spiritual and emotional challenges, it was the physical and mental demands of everyday life that proved unexpectedly difficult. Moving overseas also meant living independently for the first time after spending many years with family. Setting up a home alone in a foreign country required countless decisions and responsibilities, often leaving little time or energy outside of work to rest or continue exploring business ideas.

Language and culture presented another challenge. Although YT expected the adjustment to take time, it was frustrating not being able to communicate, connect, and serve as effectively as back home.

"I felt helpless and found myself thinking that it'd have been more “efficient” to just stay and serve back home."

Turning Point

Throughout this journey, God faithfully spoke through friends, missionaries, and church leaders. Again and again, YT was reminded that participating in God's mission, whether locally or overseas, is not a sprint but a marathon.

More importantly, YT's perspective shifted from focusing on doing to simply being.

"What God seeks is not efficiency or 'ROI' (else Jesus' time on earth might have looked very different), but rather faithful servants and willing hearts."

This has become a lesson that YT continually returns to. Living overseas has also deepened YT's appreciation for incarnational ministry. Just as God came near to humanity, YT has come to see that participating in God's mission means drawing near to people, not as a saviour or outsider, but through empathy, presence, and genuine relationships.

“With that, even my unseen, boring life takes on meaning. It helps me gain empathy and reflect just a little of how God is not a distant deity, but rather is up close and personal.”

Developments and Emerging Missional Intentionality

Although one of YT's original motivations for moving to Japan was to be a witness in the workplace, it was easy to lose sight of that purpose amidst the routines and demands of work.

However, the shift in perspective has been a continual reminder that work itself is not simply a means to an end, nor does ministry begin only after the workday is over. Instead, the workplace is where God's goodness and love can be reflected through everyday interactions. Faithful witness through daily life thus becomes all the more significant.

“Ultimately, I think that it’s not so different from being a witness at the workplace back home, although Singapore’s 18% Christian population is greatly different from Japan’s <1%, which makes it so much more vital to be a witness here.”

Recently, YT had the opportunity to share briefly about the Christian faith with a colleague during a conversation about relationships. Although the colleague had heard of Christianity, they knew very little about it. In imperfect Japanese, YT explained that the Christian understanding of God is that of a loving Father rather than merely a giver of rules; a perspective that surprised the colleague.

It was a simple conversation, but an encouraging reminder that God can use even ordinary moments to open doors for the gospel.

“I can continue to be salt and light, and always be prepared to give an answer to everyone who asks me to give the reason for the hope that I have.”

Church and Community Support

Although YT made the decision to relocate to Japan independently, his home church chose to come alongside by providing financial support, prayer, regular check-ins, and ongoing encouragement.

YT also partners with OMF, whose network provides practical support, encouragement, and community while serving overseas. Knowing that both the church and OMF continue to pray faithfully has been a constant source of encouragement throughout this journey.

Reflection and Encouragement

Serving overseas has reminded YT that missions are often imagined as doing big things; organising events, leading programmes, or going on mission trips. Yet God is just as present in the ordinary rhythms of everyday life.

Reflecting on the example of Jesus, YT has come to value what is often described as incarnational ministry.

"Missional living is not just doing good works or preaching the gospel, but living a faithful life that seeks after the one true God, and simply being present with his people and loving them as they are, rather than seeking to come with the 'right answer' or agenda."

It is a lesson YT continues to learn: to seek God not only in the exciting or the extraordinary, but also in the ordinary, and to remain faithful wherever God has placed them.

ARCHETYPE 2:

THE
BI-VOCATIONAL
WITNESS

A person who gains employment overseas, and sees their vocation as an integral part of their witness for Christ.

2

This archetype describes individuals who carry a clear and intentional call to make disciples of Christ in an overseas, long-term cross-cultural context. They enter the field through regular employment in an existing organization, using their professional role not as a cover, but as a holistic expression of gospel engagement. Employment here may also include those serving on a voluntary basis in an effectively full-time capacity. It may also include those ‘employed’ as students, or as home-makers (e.g. the spouse of one who is working in an employed professional role).

Their influence is primarily relational - within workplace networks and local communities. Over time, their embeddedness as an employee creates natural, credible access for evangelism, discipleship, and witness. In contexts where missionary visas are restricted, their employment also provides the legal and social legitimacy to remain and build trust. Critically, they view their work as genuinely meaningful in itself - not simply a means to an end.

Key Characteristics

  • Enters with a clear, pre-existing missional call.
  • Enters field through employment in an existing organisation.
  • Views vocation and witnessing for Christ as integrated.
  • May serve in restrictive or access-limited contexts.
  • Likely operates within a sending or accountability structure.
  • Influence flows primarily through workplace and community relationships.

There are two sub-categories within this pathway: Exploring Bi-Vocational Witness (2A) and Ready Bi-Vocational Witness (2B).

A second turning point came through an unexpected door. Broken families among the local staff drew them into pre-marital and marriage counselling. During one session, there was a demonic manifestation. Led by the Holy Spirit, they cast out evil spirits in the name of Jesus, with their children supporting them through prayer and worship.

“That experience made us far more aware of the reality of spiritual warfare and the power of God, and we became much more intentional in prayer.”

Their church later sent them for training with Ellel Ministries. The fruit has been lasting - transformation in marriages and families, and some of those they counselled went on to disciple others.

Developments and Emerging Missional Intentionality

Having an identity as professional coaches opened doors that had not previously existed. K and T found themselves coaching senior leaders and managers of secular companies - building relationships as they empowered people to become better leaders, and creating natural opportunities for the gospel.

The coaching work was real and professional - but it was always in service of something larger. Alongside the marketplace work, they mentored and coached small group leaders and church leaders in disciple making. The Lord opened further doors to support young local leaders in cross cultural mission training.

Eventually, they were certified as coach trainers, equipping marketplace and church leaders to become more empowering in their own leadership.

“Some of the young men and women we mentored and coached gained clarity of God’s calling on their lives and followed that call in a very difficult environment. We are thankful that today, some of them are leaders and pastors in the house churches.”

Church and Community Support

K and T are grateful to their sending church, which provided their living expenses, and to the BAM company, which provided the work visa, their housing, and education for their children. They are equally grateful for friends and supporters who prayed and gave sacrificially.

The road was not without institutional disappointment. Their church field leader felt they had not met his expectations, and they were later re-categorised from church missionaries to bi-vocational missionary status, resulting in lower financial support.

In spite of this, they reflect with grateful hearts:

“The tension we felt between the expectations of our sending church and the BAM company could have been resolved had we requested both parties to sit down early, discuss, and establish a formal agreement on our roles and their respective expectations.”

“Through it all, God has been faithful in providing for us - through health challenges and unpredictable circumstances alike.”

Reflection and Encouragement

Looking back, K and T see God’s hand even in the hardest chapters. The initial setback of not being able to work with the local church in disciple making was, they now believe, a blessing - one that forced them into more authentic roles suited to the creative access environment.

For those heading into similar tensions between sending churches and field organisations, they offer a practical word.

“The tension we felt between the expectations of our sending church and the BAM company could have been resolved had we requested both parties to sit down early, discuss, and establish a formal agreement on our roles and their respective expectations.”

Re-entry to Singapore was a very difficult season - caring for ageing parents and supporting their children through their own transition. Yet it was also necessary and pivotal.

In the midst of that season, God led them to start a coaching company, which gave them an identity and helped them refocus on their bi-vocational calling. Today, they serve as missionary coaches within their church, supporting new missionaries in their transition to the field and providing re-entry debriefing and coaching for returned missionaries as they transition back to life, work, and ministry in Singapore.

How a Doctor and a Teacher Gave Their Best Years to an Unreached People Group.

Case Study 2

“But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you.” - Matthew 6:33

About S & Y

S and Y are first-generation Christians who came to faith in their teenage years and have never looked back. Shaped by the Navigators in the mid-1980s, they caught the heart of God for His kingdom early and have been walking toward the unreached ever since.

Their call to be missionaries was clear from the start: to make disciples among an unreached people in a Creative Access Nation. Their professions - medicine and teaching - were the means that allowed them to stay on in a nation where no missionary visa existed.

Background and Journey

S and Y came to faith in Jesus in their teenage years. Being followed up by the Navigators in the mid-1980s, they caught the heart of God to labour for His kingdom, starting right where they were, in the Navigators’ teenagers ministry. Back then, missions was still something at the back of their minds. They were focused on reaching out and discipling teenagers in Singapore.

But God was already laying deeper foundations. Both first-generation Christians, they were on fire from the moment they embraced the gospel. By the late 1980s, as various missionaries came and shared their stories at Navigator camps, they began to see what God was doing around the world. Something stirred.

The defining moment came during the closing response at one of those camps. S walked to the front and gave his heart fully.

“God, wherever You lead me, I’ll go. Whatever You ask me to do, I’ll do.”

In His grace, God opened the door for S to study medicine and for Y - his wife-to-be - to receive a teaching scholarship. What began as God’s providence became, in time, a deliberate strategy: their professions were not careers to be set aside for ministry, but the very means by which ministry became possible.

Transition and Challenges

Graduating from university and entering their respective vocations brought real pressure. Work was demanding. Moving from the Navigators into a church setting after getting married brought further adjustments. With the weight of two professional careers and a new marriage, the temptation was real - to push the call to missions to the periphery, or to some unspecified point in the future when life was more settled.

“It was very tempting to push the call to missions to the periphery or some other space in the future.”

The road ahead could easily have narrowed into the comfortable and the convenient.

Turning Point

What kept them on course was community - mentors and church leaders who prayed for them, encouraged them, and refused to let the call go cold. Without that, S reflects, the road to missions would have been impossible.

But the moment that brought absolute clarity came through grief. A close friend of S’s died suddenly during an overseas military training - a man in the prime of his life, gone without warning. S and Y had already been convicted not to give God their leftover years - not to serve Him in missions only when careers were established or financial security was achieved. The news of his friend’s passing sharpened everything.

“God’s timing is mysterious. The time to serve Him wholeheartedly is now.”

The loss of his friend did not only produce despair. It produced clarity.

Developments and Emerging Missional Intentionality

From that point on, S and Y followed whatever direction God led them in preparation for the field. They attended a tentmaking course organised by Discipleship Training Center and completed their church’s in-house one-year internship programme. They made deliberate, countercultural financial choices - convicted by the motto: “Live simply, so that you might simply live.” They chose to live beneath their income. At one point, with their first son newly arrived, they lived on a single church intern’s stipend - two qualified professionals, voluntarily stepping back.

“It was very counter-cultural then and really tough at times. But God was showing us that the one who puts bread on our table is not ourselves but Him.”

It was a season of leaning into God’s provision and remaining in a posture of dependence; a lesson that has stayed with them through everything that followed.

“To this day, He remains the Faithful One who keeps us in good health and provides us with the means to serve Him.”

Their pathway into the field reflected the hierarchy of their calling. They entered first on student visas, spending time learning the language and culture before anything else. They then sought the vocational footing that would allow them to remain long-term - S in medicine and Y in teaching. If a direct route into church work had been available, they would have taken it.

Church and Community Support

S and Y are clear-eyed about the debt they owe to the community around them. The support they received - from family, church, mentors, and fellow labourers - was never primarily financial. It was something harder to quantify and more sustaining.

“Through years of preparation and of going to the unreached people group in a sensitive place, we felt uplifted by the precious faith and like-minded community who walked the journey of missions with us.”

That community did not simply send them off and move on. They walked the journey together.

Reflection and Encouragement

Jim Elliot once said, “He is no fool who gives what he cannot keep to gain what he cannot lose.”

Without the sacrifices and faith lessons of their missions journey, S and Y reflect, they would never have been in a place to truly identify with the challenges their friends face in coming to Christ on the mission field. The cost of following Jesus in a context hostile to Christianity - they have seen it up close, and they know it far outweighs anything they themselves gave up to serve God.

“In fact, the cost of following Jesus in a place that’s hostile to Christianity far outweigh all that we have given up to serve God. And when we, together, see what God is doing in the mission field through the years, we cannot agree more that it has all been worth it.”

CONCLUSION

We return to a fundamental question of why we saw the need for a bi-vocational missions workers primer.

Ultimately, we want to see the Great Commission fulfilled.

Bi-vocational missions has been practised for a long time, albeit under different names (e.g., tentmaking, business as missions). However, it has received at best a mixed response from the churches, and there has been some struggle to grasp this mode of doing missions. We saw a need to provide clarity and recognition to the various forms of bi-vocational missions, and hope that the primer will be such a blessing.

We envision a future where bi-vocational missions is well received and understood by the churches. There is potential to unleash the largest cross-cultural missions work force that the church has yet seen.

We see the potential for impact at three levels.

First, the missionary. The archetypes provide a means of describing someone’s work when the term “missionary” alone does not do justice. The archetypes enable a Christian who wants to participate in missions to understand multiple streams for doing so.

Second, there is impact for the local church.

Understanding bi-vocational missions has substantial implications for how a local church disciples its members.

Imagine if every church discipled believers to the point where they recognized that (a) we are a royal priesthood, (b) that the kingdom of God covered every sphere of life, (c) that we bear witness to Christ both in words and in actions, (d) and that we want people to know Jesus and to follow Him.

In such a church, even if believers have not discerned a specific call to missions, they would be ready to respond to the opportunities that arise wherever they go in the world (this is Archetype 1 - the Vocational Witness).

Some may discern a call to missions, and intentionally order their lives towards going via the bi-vocational route. They may go as professionals or full-time home makers (Archetype 2 - the Bi-Vocational Witness).

A few may elect to pursue a business or social enterprise that brings holistic gospel transformation to people, and open the door for others to follow (Archetype 3 - the Bi-Vocational Entrepreneur).

Some may have been sent first as a missionary doing full-time church-related work, and later on switched to taking up some form of marketplace work (Archetype 4 - Bi-Vocational Missionary).

We hope that the church will go on to develop ways of helping these workers get sent and serve effectively in the mission field.

The third level of impact is the missions ecosystem.

We see how every organ of the Church plays a meaningful part in missions-sending: the local churches, the para-church organizations, and the marketplace Christian groups and networks.

Bi-vocational missions requires these various parts to collaborate, develop strategies and best practices, and adapt if necessary, to enable the next wave of missions workers to go and to be effective in their ministry.

We look forward to the day when churches make no sharp distinction between ‘traditional’ and ‘bi-vocational’ missions, because the latter has become normalized.

May the Lord bless the work of our hands.
